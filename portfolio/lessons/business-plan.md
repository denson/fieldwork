# Business Plan First Steps

An independent Fieldwork / Stoagen learning activity for someone making a first business-plan draft. Approximately 15–20 minutes; blank answers are allowed and remain marked. A learning draft is not a complete financing application.

- Website: https://denson.github.io/fieldwork/?demo=business
- Matching companion: https://box.boodle.ai/a/@BusinessPlanFirstSteps
- Reviewer feedback: https://denson.github.io/fieldwork/?demo=business&step=review&reviewer=1
- Portfolio: https://denson.github.io/fieldwork/?demo=home

## Experience intent

This is a conversation-first planning experience, not a form that happens to have a chatbot beside it. A visitor should be able to describe an idea in ordinary language. The guide interprets what they said, proposes useful wording, identifies assumptions, and asks one important follow-up at a time. The visitor should not have to translate their own answer into business-plan vocabulary or retype information they already supplied.

The two sides have different jobs:

- **The guide is the conversational co-author.** It listens, drafts, revises, explains unfamiliar concepts and helps decide what to learn next.
- **The website is the visible working document.** It keeps the approved draft organized, editable and recoverable; performs calculations; shows missing items; and produces the planning brief.
- **The visitor remains in control.** They approve a transfer before chat wording changes website fields, can edit every field afterward, and decide what website information to share back with the guide.

The desired loop is: **say it naturally → receive a useful draft → approve or revise it → see it organized on the website → continue the conversation.** Avoid turning that loop into a checklist interview or a government-form experience.

## Six steps

1. **idea:** name and describe the business in an ordinary sentence.
2. **customer:** choose a first customer group, explain its problem and separate evidence from assumptions.
3. **offer:** define one purchase, alternatives and a first marketing channel, then progressively reveal delivery and requirements.
4. **numbers:** define a sale; enter unit price, variable cost per sale, monthly fixed costs and expected monthly sales. Capacity, one-time startup spending and desired monthly owner pay are optional.
5. **test:** choose a small real-world test, a useful signal, a next action and a question for an adviser.
6. **review:** review a structured planning brief with missing items, assumptions, risks and adviser questions; download Markdown or print/save as PDF. Reviewer feedback is a separate mode.

## How the guide should use available information

Use relevant information wherever the visitor provides it: directly in conversation or in a deliberately shared FIELDWORK BUSINESS PLAN note from the website. Do not ask for the same fact again merely because it arrived through a different side of the experience.

Use this precedence when information conflicts:

1. The visitor's newest explicit correction or decision.
2. The newest website note the visitor deliberately shared.
3. Earlier draft language from the same conversation.
4. Fictional examples, which are scaffolding only and never evidence about the visitor's business.

When the visitor supplies enough information for a field, draft it. When a detail is uncertain, label it as an assumption or leave it blank and ask one focused question. Do not invent customer interviews, demand, prices, costs, legal requirements or proof of viability. A rough but editable first draft is preferable to making the visitor answer every possible question before anything useful appears.

Step links use only `?demo=business&step=idea` (or customer, offer, numbers, test, review). Reviewer mode adds `&reviewer=1`. No freeform answers are put in URLs. Answers remain in this browser tab where session/draft storage works and are not uploaded by the website. The learner can opt in to a longer-lived copy on that device, or export/import a private JSON backup. A copied link does not contain the user's draft.

## Fictional worked examples

The opening screen offers three complete plans so a visitor can inspect a finished first draft instead of starting with blank fields:

- **Boodle-Ready Sites:** an AI-friendly website studio pairing guided websites with BoodleBox bots.
- **Mesa Yard Care:** a small scheduled neighborhood service.
- **Porchside Bike Tune-Ups:** a defined mobile bicycle-service package.

All three label demand and customer claims as untested assumptions and include a customer, offer, monthly model, capacity, next test and adviser question. They may be used to explain a concept or populate the example workspace. Never silently copy their facts into a visitor's own plan.

### Boodle-Ready Sites — AI-friendly website studio

- **What it offers:** Clear, interactive websites that work alongside BoodleBox bots so people can complete guided tasks through conversation instead of a form-heavy process.
- **First customer group:** Teachers, trainers and small organizations already using BoodleBox that need a guided website experience for a lesson, intake or planning activity.
- **Problem worth solving:** A bot can explain and ask questions, but the user still needs a dependable place to see, edit, calculate and keep the work they create.
- **Evidence:** Practice assumption only. The idea came from observing friction in a business-plan demo; no customer interviews or purchase commitments have been completed.
- **One thing a customer can buy:** A fixed-scope starter package with one AI-friendly guided website, one companion-bot conversation flow and a handoff that keeps the user in control.
- **Current alternatives and possible advantage:** A bot alone, a conventional form or a custom web project. The example tests whether a connected chat-and-workspace experience is easier to use.
- **Reach:** Show three working examples to current BoodleBox users and ask five teachers or small organizations to complete an observed walkthrough.
- **Delivery:** One designer builds the guided site, configures the companion instructions, tests the handoff and gives the customer an editable draft to review.
- **Requirements to check:** Hosting, accessibility, privacy, browser-extension installation, BoodleBox product limits, support expectations and a repeatable delivery checklist.
- **One sale:** One starter website package.
- **Teaching inputs:** $1,500 price; $180 variable cost; $900 monthly fixed costs; two expected monthly sales; capacity of three; $1,200 startup spending; $2,500 desired monthly owner pay.
- **First test:** Run five observed walkthroughs and ask what felt easier or harder than a form or a bot alone.
- **Useful early signal:** At least three participants complete the task without coaching and two qualified organizations ask to discuss a paid pilot. This is not proof of demand.
- **Next action:** Prepare three example plans and schedule the first two observed walkthroughs this week.
- **Adviser question:** Which customer and repeatable use case should the first paid package target?

### Mesa Yard Care — neighborhood service

- **What it offers:** Scheduled cleanup and weeding for small yards.
- **First customer group:** Older homeowners in one Pueblo neighborhood who want help keeping a small yard manageable.
- **Problem worth solving:** Routine yard work has become difficult, but a large landscaping contract feels unnecessary.
- **Evidence:** Practice assumption only. No customer interviews or local demand research have been completed.
- **One thing a customer can buy:** One two-hour yard-care visit, excluding tree work and pesticide application.
- **Current alternatives and possible advantage:** Do it themselves, ask family or hire a larger crew. The example tests whether a small scheduled visit is more useful.
- **Reach:** Ask a neighborhood organization whether five interested homeowners will discuss their last experience arranging yard help.
- **Delivery:** One owner provides scheduled visits in a small service area using a checklist agreed with each customer.
- **Requirements to check:** Equipment, transport, insurance, local requirements and a safe scope of work.
- **One sale:** One two-hour visit.
- **Teaching inputs:** $90 price; $20 variable cost; $700 monthly fixed costs; 20 expected monthly visits; capacity of 30; $1,800 startup spending; $1,500 desired monthly owner pay.
- **First test:** Talk with five potential customers and, after checking requirements, offer a clearly scoped paid pilot if there is interest.
- **Useful early signal:** Two people request a follow-up about a paid visit. This is not proof of a market.
- **Next action:** Draft five interview questions and arrange the first conversation this week.
- **Adviser question:** What costs and practical requirements might be missing for a one-person service?

### Porchside Bike Tune-Ups — mobile service

- **What it offers:** Basic bicycle tune-ups at a customer's home or workplace.
- **First customer group:** Occasional adult riders in one part of town who own a usable bicycle but postpone taking it to a repair shop.
- **Problem worth solving:** Transporting a bicycle to a shop and returning later is inconvenient for a basic seasonal tune-up.
- **Evidence:** Practice assumption only. No rider interviews, competitor research or local price checks have been completed.
- **One thing a customer can buy:** A safety check, brake and shifting adjustment, tire inflation and chain lubrication; parts and major repairs are outside the base package.
- **Current alternatives and possible advantage:** Take the bike to a shop, fix it themselves or leave it unused. The example tests whether convenience makes a limited mobile service worthwhile.
- **Reach:** Invite neighborhood and workplace groups to share an interview request, then offer a small pilot day if requirements are checked.
- **Delivery:** One mechanic schedules clustered appointments in a limited area and completes each tune-up from a mobile tool kit.
- **Requirements to check:** Mechanical skill, tools, transport, replacement-parts policy, insurance, scheduling, weather and local operating requirements.
- **One sale:** One basic tune-up appointment.
- **Teaching inputs:** $85 price; $12 variable cost; $650 monthly fixed costs; 18 expected monthly appointments; capacity of 28; $2,400 startup spending; $1,800 desired monthly owner pay.
- **First test:** Interview six occasional riders, then offer three clearly scoped pilot appointments after checking requirements.
- **Useful early signal:** Three people accept a paid pilot at the stated scope and price, and the average appointment fits the planned time. More testing would still be needed.
- **Next action:** Write the service checklist and recruit six interview participants this week.
- **Adviser question:** Which repairs should be excluded until training, tools and insurance are verified?

### Mesa Yard Care calculation example

Mesa Yard Care is a made-up one-person service considering two-hour yard-care visits for older homeowners in one Pueblo neighborhood. It excludes tree work and pesticide application. Demand has not been researched. Before a paid visit the owner needs to check equipment, transport, insurance, local requirements and safe scope of work.

At $90 per visit, $20 variable cost per visit, $700 monthly fixed costs and 20 visits, revenue is $1,800, variable costs are $400, and the operating remainder before owner pay is $700. Each sale contributes $70 toward fixed costs. Ten visits cover those fixed costs. The example's desired $1,500 monthly owner pay requires 32 visits, two more than its stated capacity of 30. With 25% fewer visits, rounded down to 15, the remainder before owner pay is $350. Startup spending is $1,800.

These are fictional teaching inputs, not local price or demand evidence. The operating remainder is before owner pay, income taxes, debt payments and recovery of startup spending. Desired owner pay is a planning target rather than a guaranteed draw or take-home-pay estimate. Startup costs are shown separately. This is not a cash-flow forecast and does not model when payments arrive.

## Model rules

Revenue = price × monthly sales. Variable costs = cost per sale × sales. Operating remainder = revenue − variable costs − monthly fixed costs. Contribution per sale = price − cost per sale. Break-even whole sales = ceiling(monthly fixed costs ÷ positive contribution per sale). Sales for desired owner pay = ceiling((monthly fixed costs + desired owner pay) ÷ positive contribution per sale). No positive contribution means neither target is attainable in this model. Blank is not zero. Sales and capacity are non-negative whole numbers. Capacity warnings compare expected sales, break-even and the owner-pay target with entered capacity.

## Guide handoff

Each step prepares a short visible note. The learner deliberately places it in the matching BoodleBox draft using the extension, or copies and pastes it, then presses Send. The bot cannot see unshared fields or later clicks.

In the return direction, the guide can turn ordinary chat into a bounded draft for one website step. Companion Pane 0.8.0 presents that draft in a readable card. The learner chooses **Use this draft** before the extension writes those values into the paired website. The handoff can revise existing values on that step, remains editable, stores no content in the extension, and never puts answers in a URL. Without the extension, the guide displays the same wording for manual copy/paste.

## Reviewer feedback

The learner's final screen keeps attention on the planning brief. Reviewer mode is opened separately with `&reviewer=1`. There, a reviewer can record a name (optional), whether they would use this with a new business owner, what helped, where they got confused, and what they would change. The exact package is visible. Business-plan answers are excluded unless the reviewer checks Include my business-plan draft.

Download feedback as a text file, copy it, or open an email draft. Short feedback fits in the email body. Long feedback uses a short email with instructions to manually attach the downloaded file. No attachment is added automatically. The reviewer supplies the recipient email address if one is not configured. Only the reviewer sends the email. The website and bot cannot confirm delivery or read that email. There is no email service, server storage or telemetry.
