The question worth asking

Why keep watch when most days are quiet?

Destructive tsunamis are rare, but a single event can kill thousands. NOAA estimates that tsunamis causing damage or deaths on distant shores occur about twice per decade worldwide. That is a historical average, not a schedule. NOAA/NWS background ↗

USGS earthquake monitoring and NOAA tsunami warnings work together to protect vulnerable coasts. Their value depends on being ready before the exceptional day arrives.

02 / How warnings work

From a moving seafloor to a community taking action.

Many earthquake-generated tsunamis begin when sudden seafloor movement displaces water. Not every earthquake generates a tsunami. How tsunamis form ↗

These jobs overlap. NOAA may issue an initial alert while ocean measurements are still arriving.

The U.S. has exposed coastlines.

Alaska, Hawaii, the West Coast, and Pacific and Caribbean territories face substantial tsunami hazards. Low-lying communities and harbors can be threatened by local events or waves crossing an ocean. NOAA/NWS coastal hazards ↗

A nearby tsunami can outrun an official alert.

In a coastal hazard zone, strong or long shaking, unusual sea movement, or an ocean roar can be a natural warning. Protect yourself during shaking; when you can move safely, follow evacuation routes to high ground or inland. Do not wait for an official alert. NOAA/NWS safety guidance ↗

03 / What readiness costs

The public pays for readiness on quiet days, too.

Both NOAA and USGS maintain sensor networks with partners at substantial taxpayer expense. Public funding keeps instruments reporting, communications working, and specialists ready to interpret an event.

NOAA’s DART instruments are designed for tsunami detection. USGS seismic networks also support earthquake response, shaking alerts, engineering, and hazard research. USGS network mission ↗

A warning depends on this work being done before the earthquake happens.

Deep ocean / NOAA

39 NOAA DART systems

Each system pairs a pressure recorder on the seafloor with a moored surface buoy. It detects changes in the water column as a tsunami passes. NOAA’s National Data Buoy Center operates and maintains this U.S. network; other countries also contribute observations.

What needs funding between disasters?

Equipment and field work

Seismic stations need sensor upgrades, calibration, and continuing maintenance, including at remote sites. That work keeps their measurements useful to USGS and NOAA.

USGS laboratory priorities, 2026 ↗

Ocean servicing and power

DART equipment must be deployed and recovered at sea. Battery packs power the underwater recorder and buoy; moorings hold the buoy in position. Batteries, electronics, and communications require monitoring. The DART II design includes duplicate buoy electronics for backup.

NOAA engineering and recovery details (PDF) ↗

Communications and data quality

Staff check instrument calibration, battery condition, buoy location, and the delivery of observations. Missing or delayed data need attention while the system is still on watch.

NOAA data-management responsibilities, page 21 (PDF) ↗

People and forecasting

NOAA’s warning centers monitor 24 hours a day, every day. Specialists need maintained computing systems and forecast models to turn incoming measurements into useful warnings.

NOAA’s continuous watch ↗ · Forecasting work ↗

The system in action / July 2025

Measurements become time to act.

After the M8.8 Kamchatka earthquake, NOAA says ocean observations helped update forecasts that provided the U.S. West Coast 8–12 hours of lead time. At Crescent City, California, coastal managers issued evacuation orders hours before the waves arrived.

That was a distant event. A nearby tsunami can arrive much sooner.

Read NOAA’s September 2025 account ↗

What the historical evidence implies

In a severe event threatening populated U.S. coasts, timely warnings and effective evacuation could save many thousands of lives. The actual benefit depends on warning time, exposure, and people’s ability to act. NOAA credits the connected warning and evacuation system with limiting deaths outside Japan in 2011. 04 / Community Budget Challenge Fictional town

What will your town pay for?

You’re advising the council of fictional Riverton. Recommend a plan that improves life in town, keeps its investments working, and leaves enough money to handle a difficult year.

Read the model’s rules ↗

A public budget is a set of choices.

Residents want better libraries, safer roads, welcoming parks, flood protection, and prepared emergency teams. Each has a good case for funding. Your job is to decide how much to invest in each—and explain the tradeoffs to the people who live here.

There is no single winning allocation. A useful plan has a clear purpose, covers its costs in every year, and acknowledges what it leaves undone. This exercise calculates costs; it leaves the value of those choices for you to discuss.

Where does your money come from?

You start with $12 million available in year 1 for new investments. The town’s existing day-to-day services are already funded separately. This is an additional investment fund, not Riverton’s entire budget.

In each of years 2–5, another $1 million enters the fund after existing services are paid for. That makes $16 million over five years, but the later money is not available to spend on day one.

Opening day is only the beginning.

New facilities and equipment need ongoing upkeep. Each investment adds an annual cost, starting in year 1, and the required amount rises 3% each year.

The upkeep slider controls how much of that requirement you pay. 100% means fully funding the upkeep your chosen projects need—not spending your whole budget on maintenance. Paying less leaves more cash now, but lowers the model’s equipment-condition index over time.

What is the reserve?

Your reserve is money left in the fund after that year’s costs. It carries forward into the next year. Keeping some in reserve gives the town room to respond when things go wrong.

A negative reserve means the plan cannot pay its bills as written. You must change your choices or identify additional funding. A positive balance in year 5 does not erase a shortfall in an earlier year.

Why try the storm?

Turn on a storm in year 3 to add an unexpected repair bill. In this simplified model, flood protection, emergency readiness, and well-maintained equipment reduce that bill. They do not make it disappear.

The town, costs, and storm are fictional teaching assumptions. The exercise helps you explore preparedness and future obligations; it does not predict real flood damage or tell you which public service matters most.
